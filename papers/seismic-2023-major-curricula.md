# The biology curriculum students actually take

Fewer than half of even the most advantaged students follow a biology major's intended course sequence, and the
students who stay on it, and finish a biology degree, are disproportionately the advantaged ones.

## Question

Is the course sequence a biology curriculum intends the one students actually take, and does moving through it
affect students of different backgrounds the same way?

## Data

- Two Great Lakes research universities, about 31,000 undergraduates each
- First-year, non-transfer students from fall 2012 to 2016 cohorts, followed for six years
- Enrollments, majors, degrees and demographic records

## Approach

- Systemic Advantage Index (0 to 4) to group students
- Educational process mining (process discovery and conformance checking with bupaR) to trace course-taking
  sequences through biology and chemistry
- Outcomes: biology degree, other STEM degree, non-STEM degree, or left or still enrolled

## Findings

- At one institution, only 46.6% of biology-intending students took the first biology course, and the most
  common path skipped it [Results, RQ1]
- At the other, students on allowed biology sequences were 33% of the most advantaged group but 24.3% of the
  least advantaged; 18.6% of the least advantaged moved to non-STEM degrees [Figure 3]
- In chemistry, the standard path to a biology degree covered 20.4% of the most advantaged students but 8.2% of
  the least; five common paths for the least advantaged involved retaking courses, none for the most [Figure 5]

## Why it matters

The authors argue curricula aren't neutral: rigid, hierarchical sequences push out less advantaged students. They
call for less hierarchical sequences and asset-based teaching.
