# Grade penalties and gender gaps at Michigan

At the University of Michigan, large STEM lecture courses graded students lower than their records elsewhere
predicted, and graded women lower than comparable men. Labs did neither. In physics, the gap came from timed
exams rather than homework or in-class work.

## Question

Do large introductory courses systematically grade women lower than men with similar academic records, and does
this differ between STEM lectures, STEM labs and non-STEM courses?

## Data

- University of Michigan registrar records, fall 2008 to winter 2015
- 116 large courses (over 200 students per term), 627,998 student-course pairs [Section II]

## Approach

- Grade anomaly: course grade minus GPA in other courses; a course's average shows a grade penalty or bonus
- Gendered performance difference: women's average grade anomaly minus men's
- Robustness checks with regression, optimal matching and LASSO-selected covariates; bootstrap uncertainties
- For physics and mathematics, a breakdown by grade component (exams, homework, in-class work)

## Findings

- STEM lecture courses gave an average penalty of 0.22 letter grades, up to 0.62 [Section III]
- In first-year gateway lectures in physics, chemistry, biology and mathematics, the mean gender gap was −0.18
  letter grades; women's average penalty in these courses was 0.51 letter grades [Section III, Fig. 4]
- Across STEM courses the mean gender gap was about −0.11, and about −0.10 after matching [Section III]
- STEM labs gave a small average bonus (+0.12) with essentially no gender gap [Section III]
- Adding ACT scores and high-school GPA shrank the gaps but didn't remove them [Section III.A]
- Timed exams were about 70% of lecture grades; in physics the gap came from exams, while women did slightly
  better on homework and in-class work [Section III.B]

## Why it matters

The gaps can push students, especially women, away from STEM. The authors suggest scalable responses: values
affirmation, more authentic assessment, and campus review of grading norms.
