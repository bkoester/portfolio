# Systemic advantage and grade penalties at six universities

At six large research universities, each additional systemic advantage a student holds (being male, white, not
low-income, or continuing-generation) goes with a smaller grade penalty in the biggest introductory STEM
courses. The pattern holds on every campus.

## Question

How are students distributed by systemic advantage at each university, and does the number of advantages relate
to grades in large introductory STEM courses?

## Data

- Six public research universities (19,000–42,000 undergraduates each)
- Fall 2009 to spring 2019; the 10 largest first-year STEM lecture courses at each campus, 60 courses in all
- 227,413 students (one enrollment each, from 386,035); data stayed on each campus

## Approach

- Systemic Advantage Index (SAI), 0 to 4: a count of four advantages
- Outcome: grade anomaly, the course grade minus the student's GPA in all other courses; negative means a
  grade penalty
- Regression of grade anomaly on SAI at each institution; t-tests of SAI 4 against SAI 0–3

## Findings

- Most students held three or four advantages (57% to 78% by campus); students with none were 6% or fewer
  [Table 3]
- Every group averaged a grade penalty, from −0.24 to −0.94 grade points, and penalties grew as advantages fell
  [Table 6]
- Each additional advantage was linked to a smaller penalty at all six universities (slopes 0.044 to 0.104 grade
  points per advantage, all p < 0.001) [Table 7]

## Why it matters

The same pattern at six peer institutions points to systemic inequity in gateway courses rather than individual
deficits. The authors call on instructors and leaders to examine how their own courses produce the penalties.
