# Catching a supernova in its first days

A short X-ray flash in a nearby galaxy led to spectra of a new supernova less than two days later. The
explosion time is known unusually well, so the event gives a rare early look at a stripped-star supernova.

## Question

What kind of supernova followed the bright X-ray transient seen in NGC 2770, and how does it compare with
supernovae linked to gamma-ray bursts?

## Data

- Swift X-ray detection of the transient on 2008 January 9.56 UT, lasting about 10 minutes [Sec. I]
- Spectra from VLT/FORS2, NOT/ALFOSC and WHT/ISIS, plus a high-resolution VLT/UVES spectrum [Sec. I; Table 1]
- Imaging from NOT, VLT, the Liverpool Telescope and UKIRT [Sec. I; Table 2]

## Approach

- Took spectra starting 1.75 days after the X-ray transient and followed the spectral evolution [Sec. I-II]
- Measured line velocities with SYNOW models [Sec. II.2, Fig. 4]
- Estimated dust extinction from sodium absorption, interstellar bands and the X-ray absorbing column [Sec. II.3]
- Compared the light curve with other supernovae and estimated the nickel mass [Sec. III]

## Findings

- The early spectrum was flat with an unusual W-shaped feature, gone by 3.5 days after the transient
  [Sec. II.1-II.2, Fig. 3]
- Helium lines appeared from January 17 on, making it a Type Ib supernova [Abstract; Sec. II.2]
- Host redshift measured as 0.0070 ± 0.0009, a distance of 29.9 Mpc [Sec. I]
- Adopted reddening E(B-V) = 0.8 mag [Sec. II.3]
- About 0.09 solar masses of nickel-56, about a factor of 5 less than in gamma-ray-burst supernovae [Sec. III]

## Why it matters

The authors call it the first Type Ib supernova with a tightly pinned explosion time; whether such X-ray events
and gamma-ray bursts form one family is left open.
