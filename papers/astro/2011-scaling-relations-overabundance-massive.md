# Too many massive clusters, too early?

Hubble lensing masses for distant clusters, at redshift about 1 and beyond, track their X-ray temperatures
closely, but the heaviest few are rarer than standard cosmology allows.

## Question

How do lensing masses of z >~ 1 clusters relate to X-ray temperature and velocity dispersion, and are the most
massive ones consistent with Lambda-CDM?

## Data

- 22 high-redshift clusters imaged with HST ACS in F775W and F850LP, mostly from the HST Cluster Supernova Survey [Abstract, Sec. 2]
- Combined with five previously studied clusters for 27 in total [Abstract]
- Redshifts roughly 0.91 to 1.46; source densities about 50 to 180 per square arcminute [Table 1]

## Approach

- Measure galaxy shapes by fitting PSF-convolved elliptical Gaussians [Sec. 2.3]
- Fit SIS and NFW halos; source redshifts from Ultra Deep Field photometric redshifts [Sec. 3]
- Compare masses with X-ray temperatures and velocity dispersions [Sec. 4]
- Compute discovery odds under Lambda-CDM with an Eddington-bias correction and a conservative 100 square degrees of survey area [Sec. 5]

## Findings

- Mass-temperature slope 1.54 ± 0.23, consistent with the self-similar 3/2 [Sec. 4.2, Eq. 7]
- Normalization 20-30% lower than earlier results [Abstract]
- Mass-velocity dispersion relation consistent with equality across 23 clusters [Sec. 4.1, Fig. 24]
- Combined probability of the four most massive clusters is under 1% [Abstract, Sec. 5.4]
- CL J1226+3332 sits above the joint 95% exclusion curve for 100 square degrees [Sec. 5.5, Fig. 28]

## Why it matters

The authors frame the most massive distant clusters as a continuing tension with Lambda-CDM, discuss
non-Gaussian initial conditions as one explanation, and call for more tests at the high-mass end.
