# How much mass per unit light in clusters

By measuring both the mass (through weak gravitational lensing) and the light around SDSS groups and clusters, the paper finds that the mass-to-light ratio rises with cluster mass and levels off at a common value on large scales. That large-scale value ties directly to the average matter density of the universe.

## Question

How does the ratio of excess mass to excess light around clusters depend on radius and on cluster mass, and what does its large-scale value say about Omega_m?

## Data

- MaxBCG clusters at z = 0.1–0.3 from SDSS Data Release 4, using N200 ≥ 10 clusters plus N200 ≥ 3 systems [Sec. 3; Sec. 3.1].
- Systems span small groups (M200 ~ 5×10^12 h^-1 M_sun) to clusters (M200 ~ 10^15 h^-1 M_sun) [Abstract].
- 12 richness bins and 16 luminosity bins [Tables 1–2; Sec. 6.4].
- Light traced by galaxies with L > 10^9.5 h^-2 L_sun [Sec. 3.2].

## Approach

- Measure mass profiles from weak-lensing shear and light profiles from galaxy–cluster cross-correlation with background subtraction [Sec. 2].
- Deproject both to three dimensions and take their ratio as a function of radius [Sec. 2; Eq. 1].
- Fit how the ratio at r200 scales with mass and its asymptotic value at large radius [Sec. 9].

## Findings

- (ΔM/ΔL)200 rises with mass as a power law of index 0.33 ± 0.02 [Abstract; Sec. 9.7; Fig. 9].
- For small groups (ΔM/ΔL)200 is well below the asymptotic value; for large clusters it is consistent with it [Abstract; Sec. 10].
- Asymptotic value ⟨M/L⟩ b^-2 = 362 ± 54 h within 22 h^-1 Mpc, with about 10% calibration uncertainty [Abstract; Sec. 9.8; Fig. 10].
- Implied Omega_m b^-2 = 0.20 ± 0.03, independent of the Hubble parameter [Abstract; Sec. 10].
- Profiles cover 25 h^-1 kpc to 22 h^-1 Mpc [Abstract].

## Why it matters

The authors present it as a fully photometric link between visible light and dark matter, extendable with surveys such as DES and LSST.
