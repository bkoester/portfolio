# Finding distant supernovae by watching clusters

Pointing Hubble at massive, distant galaxy clusters found supernovae beyond redshift 1 about twice as efficiently
as a blank-field search. It also tripled the number found in dust-poor red galaxies, whose supernovae make cleaner
distance markers.

## Question

Does repeatedly imaging high-redshift galaxy clusters raise the yield of distant Type Ia supernovae, especially
those in early-type hosts with little dust?

## Data

- 25 galaxy clusters at 0.9 < z < 1.5, 23 with spectroscopic redshifts [Sec. 3; Table 1]
- 188 pre-scheduled HST/ACS orbits plus 31 NICMOS follow-up orbits, July 2005 to December 2006 [Sec. 3]
- Ground-based spectroscopy from Subaru, Keck and the VLT [Sec. 3; Sec. 4.2]

## Approach

- Revisited each cluster about every 20 to 26 days, depending on redshift, so each supernova got a light curve
  in two bands [Sec. 3]
- Accepted a candidate only when it appeared in each of the four exposures per visit [Sec. 4.1]
- Typed supernovae mostly from host-galaxy spectra and red-sequence membership [Sec. 4.2.3]

## Findings

- 39 supernova candidates found in the ACS images [Sec. 4.1]
- 16 supernovae at z > 0.95, nine of them in clusters [Abstract]
- Nine of these were in red-sequence hosts, six in clusters and three in the field [Sec. 4.3; Sec. 5]
- About twice as efficient as an HST field survey, and three times the yield in red-sequence hosts [Abstract;
  Sec. 6]
- 67 of the 188 orbits effectively served cluster supernova light curves, against 54 for field supernovae at
  z > 0.95 [Sec. 4.3]

## Why it matters

The authors say the cluster-targeting approach met its first measurable goal. They argue that an infrared cluster
survey with WFC3 could yield "a SN in every cluster" [Sec. 6].
