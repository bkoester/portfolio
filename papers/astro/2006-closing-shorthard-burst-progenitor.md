# Hunting the host of a short burst

A very brief gamma-ray burst appeared next to a massive, quiet elliptical galaxy in a galaxy cluster. Its
surroundings, and the lack of any supernova, point toward a merger of two compact stars rather than the
collapse of a massive star that causes long bursts.

## Question

What kind of galaxy hosted the short-hard burst GRB 050509b, and what does that say about the burst's origin?

## Data

- Swift BAT detection (duration ~30 ms) and XRT follow-up totalling 32.3 ks over ~21 hr [Sec. I; Sec. III]
- Early optical/IR imaging from WIYN 3.5 m and PAIRITEL 1.3 m, deep imaging from Keck I LRIS and Keck II ESI
  [Sec. II]
- Keck DEIMOS spectrum of the bright galaxy G1 and Gemini GMOS spectra of two fainter galaxies [Sec. V]

## Approach

- Re-derived the X-ray position by tying XRT sources to optical/2MASS astrometry [Sec. III.2]
- Fit the X-ray fading and the diffuse X-ray emission of the nearby cluster [Sec. III]
- Measured G1's redshift, velocity dispersion, shape and star-formation limit [Sec. V]
- Estimated chance-alignment odds and compared limits to afterglow, supernova and magnetar models [Sec. IV; VI; VII]

## Findings

- G1 is an elliptical at z = 0.2248 ± 0.0002, offset 40 ± 13 kpc in projection from the burst [Abstract; Sec. III; Sec. V]
- About 11 fainter galaxies also fall within the X-ray error circle [Abstract]
- The host cluster's gas temperature is kT = 5.25 (+3.36/−1.68) keV [Sec. III.4]
- G1's Hα limit implies star formation below 0.1 solar masses per year [Sec. V]
- No new source to R_c ≈ 25.0 mag at 8.17 days rules out a normal Type Ia supernova at that redshift [Sec. II; Sec. VII]

## Why it matters

If the association holds, short bursts come from old stellar populations, consistent with compact-binary
mergers, a possible gravitational-wave source.
