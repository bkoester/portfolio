# A short receptor variant doesn't explain drug response

Some species, including humans, barely react in the liver to drugs called peroxisome proliferators, while rats
react strongly. This study tested whether a shortened, inactive form of the receptor these drugs act on
explains the difference, and found that it does not.

## Question

Does the level of the truncated PPARα2 mRNA, relative to the full-length PPARα1 form, account for differences
between species, or between sexes, in how the liver responds to peroxisome proliferators?

## Data

- Human tissues: liver, kidney, testes, heart, small intestine and smooth muscle [Abstract]
- Human liver samples, n = 18 for the PPARα1-PPARα2 correlation [Abstract]
- Liver from rat, mouse, rabbit and monkey; hepatocytes from human, rat and rabbit [Abstract]

## Approach

- Measured the two spliced mRNA forms and their ratio in each species [Abstract]
- Tested the truncated form's ability to switch on a PPAR-responsive reporter in transfected cells [Abstract]
- Treated hepatocytes with the PPARα activator WY14643 and measured acyl CoA oxidase (ACO) mRNA [Abstract]
- Assay methods are not described in the abstract; the full text was not reached.

## Findings

- PPARα2 lacks the ligand-binding domain and did not activate the reporter [Abstract]
- In human liver, PPARα2 mRNA was about half the level of PPARα1, and the two correlated with r = 0.75 (n = 18) [Abstract]
- The PPARα2/PPARα1 ratio was below 0.10 in rat, rabbit and mouse liver, about 0.3 in monkey and about 0.5 in human [Abstract]
- WY14643 raised ACO mRNA sharply in rat hepatocytes but not in human or rabbit hepatocytes [Abstract]
- Rabbit liver has a low ratio yet does not respond, and male and female rats respond differently despite similar ratios [Abstract]

## Why it matters

The authors conclude that selective expression of the truncated receptor cannot explain why species and sexes
differ in their response to peroxisome proliferators, so another mechanism must be involved.
