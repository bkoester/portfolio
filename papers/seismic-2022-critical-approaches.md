# Doing quantitative equity work without deficit framing

An essay, drawn from the SEISMIC collaboration's work with institutional data, on how analyses that use student
demographics can avoid working against the equity they aim to measure.

## Question

How can quantitative STEM education research avoid reinforcing inequity, and how can critical perspectives such as
QuantCrit shape how analyses are framed, run and interpreted?

## Data

None; this is a perspective essay grounded in the authors' SEISMIC work.

## Approach

- Contrasts the assumptions common in STEM training with critical theories of knowledge
- Introduces QuantCrit and intersectionality as ways to put statistics to work for equity
- Offers eight reflective questions for researchers (Table 1), framed as prompts rather than a checklist

## Findings

- Framing results as achievement gaps can reinforce deficit thinking; study how students succeed instead
  [Table 1]
- Add demographic variables to models only with a theoretical reason; standardized test scores are a weak proxy
  for preparation [Table 1]
- Include structural measures (climate, policy, selectivity) and document how demographic categories were defined
  and changed over time [Table 1]
- Mixed or qualitative methods may reveal mechanisms that quantitative work alone misses [Table 1]

## Why it matters

Careless use of quantitative data can hide systemic causes behind stories about students. The authors present
their questions as a starting point, noting that "imperfection and improvement represent the norm of equity
analyses."
